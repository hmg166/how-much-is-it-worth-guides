A family clear-out often stalls on the same short question: "what's it worth?" This page sets four answers side by side so you can see why a melt figure, a marketplace ask, a shop offer, and an appraisal shouldn't be used as if they were the same number.

They can all be honest, and they can still sit some distance apart.

What's it worth: a side-by-side view

Face value is official and narrow. It tells you what a circulating denomination does in commerce, or what a mint stamp does as postage. It doesn't describe collector demand.

Melt is a metal calculation. It's useful for gold and silver items when weight and fineness are known. It's a floor, not a collector premium, and not automatically a scrap dealer's payout. It doesn't apply to a copper wheat cent or a clad dollar.

A listed range is a picture of current asking prices for a matching item and condition on public marketplaces. That's the everyday resale question. Asking prices can sit above sold prices. A dealer bid is usually lower still, because the dealer needs a margin.

An appraisal is a professional opinion, often for insurance or an estate. It's the right document when the decision is high stakes. It isn't a first step for every object in a cupboard.

How to keep the columns from collapsing

Always attach the question to a specific object and condition. A photo, plus a mark on the back, does that. If someone gives you a number, ask which column it came from, how current the comps are, and whether the condition matches. If the piece is rare or hard to authenticate from a picture, stop at the first look and use a specialist. If you sell ordinary items, draft a listing for eBay, Facebook Marketplace, or Gumtree from the real condition, not from the top ask you saw.

Photographs that keep the identification honest

A front photo is enough to start, and a second photo of the back, date, mint mark, serial, hallmark, tag, or model label usually tightens the match. Crop for sharpness rather than atmosphere. Coins and bills do better with both sides. Cards need front and back. Jewelry and watches need marks and the case back. Sneakers and handbags need labels, size, and the condition of edges, corners, or lining. Electronics need the model plate and a clear note of what is included.

If you can't read a mark, expect a wider range. If authenticity can't be confirmed from a picture, the first look should lead to a qualified specialist rather than to a rushed listing.
