WorthPoint Alternative: Scanner - Check an Item From a Photo

The best worthpoint alternative depends on the question you are actually trying to answer. WorthPoint is a paid research service for antiques, art, and collectibles. Its core is a price guide of past sales gathered from auction houses and online marketplaces. Its App Store listing describes that archive as nearly two decades of historical pricing data, and it also has a marks directory, a digital library, and a dictionary. That is a serious tool if you need years of sold results, or you are trying to place a maker by a stamp you cannot read.

A photo scanner is built for a different job. You take a picture of the item in front of you, confirm the identification, and then look at current public listings and an estimated sale price for that exact thing. WorthPoint alternative when you do not yet know what you have

Plenty of people use both tools, and that is often the honest answer. The split is simple. WorthPoint answers, "what have items like this sold for in the past?" You need to know what you have, type a good search, then read through the results. A photo scanner answers, "what is this, and what is it going for now?" Current listings tell you what you are competing with today. If there are many of the same thing listed right now, your price has to beat them, whatever one sold for three years ago. That is why the scanner shows the live listings and then an estimated sale, which usually lands under the typical asking price. What a scanner run is, and is not

A listed range is a spread of asking prices, not a sold-price guarantee. The scanner is not an archive of past auction results, and it does not have a marks encyclopedia. It can read a visible mark in your photo, which is often enough to get you started. If the mark still will not place, and the piece looks like pottery, silver, or furniture that needs a deep marks search, WorthPoint's directory is the specialist tool for that job. Scan first, then pay for the deep dive if the first look suggests it is worth it.

For an antiques dealer pricing stock every day, the archive can pay for itself. For someone who found a box in the attic and wants to know if any of it is worth listing, it is a lot of tool for a question a photo can answer faster. If you already know the exact name, you can still scan it and compare the current asking side with what you have already seen in sold results.

If you already researched the same piece in a sold-price tool, use the scan as a second opinion on identification and on what is listed today, not as a reason to ignore a careful archive search.
